Cornell Notes Example: What a Finished Page Looks Like

StudyAI Guides · Updated October 2026 · 6 min read

Rules are easy to nod at and hard to copy; a finished page teaches faster. Below are three complete Cornell notes examples — biology, history and maths — each with real cues, notes and a summary, and a note on what to steal from it. The pattern is always the same: questions on the left that the right side answers, and a summary at the bottom that a stranger could learn from. If you need the blank sheet itself, grab the free template; for the routine behind these pages, see how to take Cornell notes.

Example 1 — Biology: Cellular Respiration

CUES / QUESTIONS

Where does glycolysis happen — and what does it produce?

What comes out of the Krebs cycle?

Why is oxygen needed at the very end?

Roughly how much ATP in total?

NOTES — Cellular respiration

Glycolysis (cytoplasm): glucose split into 2 pyruvate. Net gain: 2 ATP. No oxygen needed.

Krebs cycle (mitochondria): pyruvate fully broken down. CO₂ released; a little ATP plus electron carriers (NADH, FADH₂) loaded up.

Electron transport chain (inner mitochondrial membrane): carriers hand over electrons; energy pumps protons; oxygen accepts the electrons at the end (forms water). This stage makes most of the ATP.

Total ≈ 30–32 ATP per glucose.

SUMMARY

Respiration releases glucose's energy in three stages: glycolysis in the cytoplasm (2 ATP, no oxygen), the Krebs cycle in the mitochondria (CO₂ out, carriers loaded), and the electron transport chain, where oxygen's arrival lets the cell cash the carriers in for most of its ~30 ATP.

What to notice: every cue is a question a teacher could actually ask, and each one lines up with exactly one block of notes. The summary names the stages in order — it is a story, not a list of leftovers.

Example 2 — History: The Causes of World War I

CUES / QUESTIONS

What does M-A-I-N stand for?

Which assassination was the trigger — who, where, when?

Why did alliances turn a local quarrel into a world war?

NOTES — Causes of WWI

Militarism: arms race, especially Britain vs Germany at sea. Alliances: Triple Entente (Britain, France, Russia) vs Triple Alliance (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy). Imperialism: rivalry over colonies. Nationalism: ethnic groups wanting their own states, empires feeling threatened.

Trigger: Archduke Franz Ferdinand assassinated in Sarajevo, 28 June 1914.

Austria-Hungary declares war on Serbia (28 July). The alliance chain pulls in Russia, then Germany, then France; Germany's invasion of Belgium brings in Britain — all within about a week.

SUMMARY

Long-term pressures — militarism, alliances, imperialism and nationalism — loaded Europe like a spring; the Sarajevo assassination released it, and the alliance system dragged every great power into war within weeks of Austria-Hungary's attack on Serbia.

What to notice: the notes use a memory hook (M-A-I-N) and keep dates attached to the events they belong to. The summary separates the long-term causes from the trigger — the distinction examiners keep testing.

Example 3 — Maths: The Quadratic Formula

CUES / QUESTIONS

When do I reach for the formula instead of factorising?

What does the discriminant tell me before I start?

Worked check: solve x² − 5x + 6 = 0.

NOTES — Quadratic formula

For ax² + bx + c = 0:  x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a. Use when factorising is not obvious or does not work.

Discriminant D = b² − 4ac: D > 0 → two real roots; D = 0 → one repeated root; D < 0 → no real roots.

Example: a=1, b=−5, c=6. D = 25 − 24 = 1. x = (5 ± 1)/2 → x = 3 or x = 2. Check: 9 − 15 + 6 = 0 ✓ and 4 − 10 + 6 = 0 ✓.

SUMMARY

The quadratic formula solves any quadratic: identify a, b, c, check the discriminant to know how many roots to expect, substitute carefully (mind the minus signs), and verify roots in the original equation.

What to notice: in maths the notes side holds a worked example, not prose, and the cues are triggers ("when do I…?", "what does … tell me?") — exactly the questions a test makes you answer.

Strong pages vs weak pages

PartWeakStrong
Cues"Cell stuff", "War dates"Questions with one clear answer sitting beside them

SummaryThe first sentence of the notes, repeatedThe whole page retold in your own words, in order

Copy the pattern, not the words: your pages should answer your teacher's likely questions, in your subjects, the same day you take the notes. The method itself is explained in what are Cornell notes, and the full routine in our Cornell notes guide.

Frequently asked questions

Can I copy these examples for my own notes?

Use them as models — that is what they are for. Your own pages will work better if the cues are your questions in your words, but the layout, spacing and summary style above are exactly what to imitate.

How many cues should a page have?

Usually three to six: one per block of notes. If a block of notes has no cue, you have nothing to revise from — and if a cue has no notes beside it, it is pointing at a gap to fill.

What does a Cornell notes page look like for maths?

Like Example 3 above: formulas and worked examples on the right, and cues that ask when a method applies and what to check — problem subjects bend the format towards triggers and checks rather than facts.
