Spaced Repetition: Review Less, Remember More

StudyAI Guides · Updated October 2026 · 7 min read

You learn a topic on Monday and feel confident. By the exam three weeks later, half of it is gone. That is the forgetting curve at work — and spaced repetition is the technique designed to defeat it. Instead of reviewing everything many times in one day (cramming), you review each item a few times, at expanding intervals, just before you would have forgotten it.

The forgetting curve, briefly

After learning something new, memory of it decays fast at first, then more slowly. Each successful review flattens the curve: the material lasts longer before the next review is needed. Reviewing too early wastes time; reviewing too late means re-learning from zero. Spacing aims for the middle — review when the memory has faded a little but not disappeared.

A schedule that works

ReviewWhenGoal
1stNext dayCatch the steepest forgetting
2nd3 days laterConfirm it survived
3rd1 week laterMove it toward long-term memory
4th2 weeks laterMaintenance
5th1 month laterExam-proofing

Adjust by performance: an item you recalled instantly earns a longer gap; an item you forgot drops back to a short gap. Good flashcard apps automate exactly this logic.

Spaced repetition only works with active recall

Spacing your re-reading helps a little. Spacing your recall helps a lot. Every review in the schedule above should be a test: question first, answer from memory, then check. If you are new to that idea, start with our active recall guide.

How to start this week

Pick one subject. Convert this week's material into flashcards using the rules in how to make flashcards. Review daily, briefly. Ten focused minutes of due cards beats an hour of random revision. Trust the queue. Do the cards the schedule gives you, not just the ones you like. Add new cards gradually. Around 10–20 new cards a day is sustainable for most students; a flood of 200 creates a review mountain within a week.

Common mistakes

Cramming the schedule. Doing all five reviews in two days is just cramming with extra steps. Skipping hard cards. Forgotten cards are the system working — they are the ones that need the short interval. Perfect-tool hunting. Students spend more time comparing apps than studying. Paper cards in three boxes (review daily / every few days / weekly) implement the same principle.

Remember: you do not rise to the level of your study hours; you fall to the level of your review schedule. A small daily queue, kept for a month, outperforms heroic weekend sessions.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best spaced repetition schedule?

A reliable beginner schedule is: review after 1 day, then 3 days, then 7 days, then 14 days, then 30 days. If you recall an item easily, lengthen the next gap; if you forget it, shorten the gap and review it again sooner.

Is spaced repetition the same as active recall?

No. Active recall is how you review (retrieving from memory). Spaced repetition is when you review (at expanding intervals). The two combine: recall practice, spaced out over time, is the most effective combination known.

Which apps use spaced repetition?

Anki is the most powerful free option and schedules reviews automatically. Quizlet and built-in flashcard apps offer simpler versions. The tool matters less than following the schedule consistently.
