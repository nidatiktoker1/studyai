The Feynman Technique: Learn Anything in 4 Steps

StudyAI Guides · Updated October 2026 · 7 min read

"If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough." That idea — associated with physicist Richard Feynman — is the whole technique. The Feynman technique is a study method where you learn a topic by pretending to teach it in the simplest possible language. It is brutally honest: the moment your explanation turns vague, you have found exactly what you do not know.

The 4 steps

Step 1: Choose one concept

Pick a single topic — not a whole chapter. "How the Krebs cycle works" is a concept; "biology unit 4" is not. Write the concept's name at the top of a blank page.

Step 2: Explain it as if teaching a beginner

Write the explanation in plain words, avoiding jargon. Where you must use a technical term, define it immediately in simple language. Use examples and analogies. Write as if your reader is a curious twelve-year-old.

Step 3: Find the gaps and go back

You will stall somewhere: a step you can name but not explain, a term you hide behind. That stall is the diagnosis. Return to your textbook or lecture for only that part, understand it, and add it to your page. Repeat until the explanation flows.

Step 4: Simplify and sharpen

Review your page and cut every remaining piece of jargon or hand-waving ("it just happens because of the process"). Replace with concrete words and one clear example. If you can, say the explanation out loud once — speaking exposes different gaps than writing.

A quick example

Topic: why the sky is blue. Jargon version: "Rayleigh scattering of shorter wavelengths." Feynman version: "Sunlight looks white but is a mix of colours. Air molecules are tiny, and they bounce the blue part of sunlight around the sky far more than the other colours — so blue light reaches your eyes from every direction, and the sky looks blue." The second version would survive an exam follow-up question; the first would not.

When to use it (and when not to)

Feynman shines for concepts and processes: mechanisms, theories, how-and-why topics in science, economics, law and medicine. It is slower for raw facts — dates, vocabulary, anatomical names. For those, use flashcards with spaced repetition. A strong study system combines both: Feynman for understanding, recall practice for retention.

Keeping a "things I don't understand" notebook

Feynman kept a notebook titled "Notebook of Things I Don't Know About". Steal the idea: every time a Feynman session exposes a gap, log it. That notebook becomes your highest-value revision list before an exam — far more useful than re-reading chapters you already understand, and a natural partner to the question column in Cornell notes.

The test: if a smart beginner could follow your written explanation and repeat it back correctly, you know the topic. Everything else is familiarity pretending to be knowledge.

Frequently asked questions

Who was the Feynman technique named after?

It is named after Richard Feynman, the Nobel Prize-winning physicist famous for explaining difficult ideas in plain language. The method reflects his study habit: he kept a notebook of things he did not yet understand and worked on them until he could explain them simply.

How is the Feynman technique different from active recall?

Active recall is retrieving facts from memory. The Feynman technique goes further: you must organise and explain a concept in simple words, which exposes gaps in understanding, not just memory. The two combine well — explain first, then flashcard the facts you stumbled on.

How long does one Feynman session take?

About 20–30 minutes per topic: pick the concept, explain it in writing, find and fix the gaps, then simplify the explanation once more.
