What Are Cornell Notes?

StudyAI Guides · Updated October 2026 · 5 min read

Cornell notes are a note-taking system in which every page is divided into three parts before you write a word: a narrow left column for cues and questions, a wide right section for the notes themselves, and a summary strip across the bottom. The division is the whole idea. Ordinary notes only record a lesson; a Cornell page is built so that the same page can later test you on it — you cover the right side, read the questions on the left, and answer from memory.

Quick facts
Invented byWalter Pauk, education professor at Cornell University, in the 1950s
Page layoutCue column (~30%, left) · Notes section (~70%, right) · Summary (bottom)
Used forLectures, classes, textbook chapters, meetings — anything you must remember later
Also calledThe Cornell method, the Cornell note-taking system

What do Cornell notes look like?

CUES / QUESTIONS

Keywords and questions about the notes beside them — written after the lesson, not during it.

NOTES

The lesson itself: main ideas, facts, diagrams and examples, in short phrases rather than full sentences.

SUMMARY

The entire page condensed into a few sentences, in your own words.

On paper the column is usually drawn about 2.5 inches (6 cm) from the left edge, with the summary starting about 2 inches (5 cm) from the bottom. You can draw it in seconds, or skip the ruler: our free Cornell notes template has the layout ready in PDF, Word and Google Docs.

What are cues in Cornell notes?

Cues are the prompts in the left column: a keyword, a term, or best of all a question whose answer sits in the notes to its right. They are written after the lesson, while you re-read your notes — that re-reading with a purpose is where much of the learning happens. A note that says "plants convert light, water and CO₂ into glucose" gets the cue "What does photosynthesis turn into glucose?" Later, that question is your self-test.

Are Cornell notes effective?

Honestly: the ruled page by itself does nothing. What makes the system work is the behaviour it forces. Writing cues means re-processing the material the same day instead of filing it away. Reciting answers from the cues is active recall — testing yourself rather than re-reading — which learning research consistently finds far more effective than highlighting or copying. And the summary makes you compress a page into your own words, which exposes anything you only half-understood. Students who skip the cues and summary are just taking ordinary notes in a fancy box, and they get ordinary results.

Who invented Cornell notes?

Walter Pauk developed the system in the 1950s while teaching at Cornell University, and later described it in his widely used book How to Study in College. His frustration was familiar: students filled notebooks during lectures and then never interacted with them again until the night before an exam. The layout was his fix — a page design that makes review part of the format instead of an afterthought. The step-by-step routine built on the layout is covered in our full Cornell notes guide.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Cornell method of note-taking?

It is the same thing as Cornell notes: Pauk's system of a cue column, a notes section and a summary on every page, used with a routine of recording notes, reducing them into cues, reciting from those cues, reflecting, and reviewing over time.

What subjects do Cornell notes work for?

Any subject taught in lessons or chapters: sciences, history, languages, law, medicine. They suit factual and conceptual material best. For problem-solving subjects like maths, students often use the notes side for worked examples and the cues for the method steps.

Can I use Cornell notes outside school?

Yes — the format is popular for meetings, training and reading non-fiction. The cues become action points and open questions; the summary becomes the decision or takeaway.

Do Cornell notes have to be handwritten?

No. Handwriting suits the slow, selective style, but the layout works typed in Word or Google Docs just as well — what matters is filling in the cues and summary, not the pen.
