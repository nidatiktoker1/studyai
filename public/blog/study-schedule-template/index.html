Study Schedule Template — Free Weekly Timetable

StudyAI Guides · Updated October 2026 · 5 min read

Most study schedules fail by Wednesday because they were drawn around wishes: six free hours a day that never existed. A schedule that survives is built the other way round — your fixed commitments go in first, and study blocks are fitted honestly into the gaps that remain. This template is that kind of grid: seven days, hour by hour from 6:00 to 22:00, with space at the top for the one goal that matters this week. It is free, in PDF and Word, with no sign-up.

How to fill it in (5 steps)

1. Fixed things first. Classes, work, travel, meals, sleep. Shade them in. What is left is your real study time — usually less than you hoped, which is exactly why this step matters.

2. Hardest subject, freshest hour. Put the subject you avoid into your most alert slot, not the tired one at the end of the day. Easy subjects can survive tired hours; hard ones cannot.

3. Work in blocks of 25–50 minutes. One block, one topic, one task — "revise photosynthesis" beats "biology". Leave gaps between blocks; back-to-back hours look impressive and produce very little.

4. Book the reviews, not just the learning. When a block teaches something new, add its review blocks now: the same topic again after 1 day, 3 days and 7 days. That spacing is what turns a good week into retained knowledge — our spaced repetition guide explains the intervals.

5. Ten-minute Sunday review. Compare the week you planned with the week you lived. Shrink the blocks you never kept, move the ones that clashed, and set next week's single main goal at the top of the fresh sheet.

Rules that keep a schedule alive

Never plan 100% of the grid. A week with no slack collapses at the first surprise. Name breaks like appointments — "walk", not blank space you later feel guilty about. Missed a block? Move it, don't double it. Doubling tomorrow's load is how one bad day becomes a dead schedule. And study actively inside the blocks — test yourself with active recall instead of re-reading, or the best-planned hours still leak away.

Frequently asked questions

Is a study schedule the same as a study timetable?

Essentially yes — "timetable" is the common word in the UK and Commonwealth, "schedule" in the US. Both mean the same weekly grid of fixed commitments and study blocks, which is what this template gives you.

How many hours a day should I schedule?

As many as your real gaps allow — for most students that is 1–3 focused hours outside class, in 25–50 minute blocks. Two honest hours beat six planned hours that never happen, because the plan only works if you trust it.

Should I use a weekly or a daily template?

Weekly for planning — it shows clashes and spreads reviews across days. If a single day is unusually heavy, sketch that day separately, but keep the weekly sheet as the master.

Can I use this template in Google Docs or Sheets?

Yes — upload the Word file to Google Drive and open it with Google Docs; the table stays editable. In Sheets it also works after conversion, though Docs keeps the printed look closer to the PDF.

What if I keep missing the same block?

The block is in the wrong place, not the wrong size. Move it to a different hour for one week before changing anything else — most "discipline problems" turn out to be timetable problems.
