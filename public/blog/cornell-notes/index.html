Cornell Notes: The Note-Taking Method Explained (With Template)

StudyAI Guides · Updated October 2026 · 7 min read

Most notes fail at the exact moment they are needed: revision week, when a notebook full of copied sentences offers no way to test what you actually know. The Cornell note-taking method, developed at Cornell University, fixes this by building the self-test into the page itself. Every page becomes both notes and a quiz.

The page layout

SectionWhereWhat goes there
Cue columnLeft, narrow (~30% of the page)Questions, keywords and prompts — written after the lesson
Notes sectionRight, wide (~70%)Main notes during the lesson: short sentences, diagrams, examples
SummaryBottom strip2–4 sentences summarising the whole page in your own words

How to take Cornell notes: the 5 Rs

1. Record

During the lecture or video, write in the notes section only. Keep sentences short, skip lines between ideas, and capture examples — they are what make abstract points memorable later.

2. Reduce

Soon after the lesson (the same day), read your notes and write cue questions in the left column: one question for each idea, phrased so its answer sits in the notes opposite. "What are the two raw materials of photosynthesis?" — not "photosynthesis notes".

3. Recite

Cover the notes section. Read each cue question and answer it out loud, in full, from memory. Uncover and check. This step is pure active recall, built into your notebook.

4. Reflect

Add your own thinking: how does this connect to earlier topics? Where would an examiner trap you? A line or two per page is enough, and it feeds the summary.

5. Review

Write the summary at the bottom from memory, then re-run the recite step on a spaced schedule — next day, next week — exactly as in our spaced repetition guide.

Common mistakes

Writing cues weeks later. The reduce step must happen while the lesson is fresh, or the questions come out shallow. Copying slides word for word. Cornell notes reward selective, own-words notes; transcription belongs in the handout, not your page. Skipping the summary. Summarising from memory is where scattered notes become one understood idea — it is also the fastest page to re-read the night before an exam. Students who want the visual version of the same idea can pair Cornell pages with the question-first rules in our flashcard guide: every cue question can become a card.

Start small: use the Cornell layout for one subject for two weeks. The habit that matters most is the same-day cue column — get that right and the rest follows.

Frequently asked questions

What are the three sections of Cornell notes?

The page is divided into a narrow left column for cues and questions, a wider right section for notes taken during the lesson, and a summary strip at the bottom where you condense the page into a few sentences in your own words.

Are Cornell notes better than normal notes?

For revision, yes — because the cue column turns your notes into a self-test. Cover the right side, answer the questions on the left, and you are doing active recall. Plain notes offer no built-in way to test yourself.

Can I use Cornell notes on a laptop or tablet?

Yes. Most note apps let you draw or type in a two-column layout, and printable Cornell templates are easy to find. The format matters more than the medium, though handwritten notes suit the method's slow, selective style well.
